Summary

The author introduces three key innovations: a continuous knot score metric that quantifies the robustness of protein knots, surpassing binary classification; ESM3-guided generation, which achieves an 87% success rate in producing knotted proteins; and the use of ESM3 embeddings to enable 93% accurate knot classification.

Strengths

The introduction clearly states why knots matter; methods are broken down clearly; code availability is promised; aligning with FM4LS objectives.
The proposed method is simple but effective – A lightweight “reward” term can be dropped into an existing ESM-3 framework to target exotic topologies.
Integrate ESM3 to generate knotted proteins and leverage ESM3 embedding for classification accuracy; results showing a huge improvement compared to previous work.
Weaknesses

Section 2.2 introduces the scoring function but lacks explanation and justification for choosing X = 10% masking and N = 16 trials.
Section 2.1 states the method can scale from a 1.5 B to a 98 B model without providing scaling requirement considerations.
Relying solely on a 93% binary accuracy metric may not prove the model truly captures knot topology; more fine-grained, topology-specific analyses are absent.
The paper says “different proteins were used for training and validation masking”, which does not sufficiently state the split strategy clearly.
The reported 87% success rate is not compared to the same dataset as recent pipelines.
Combining expectation and actual curves into one figure for comparison would be better.
Questions

Could you supply what informed the choices of X = 10% masking and N = 16 trials, and have you run sensitivity or ablation studies to quantify their effect on knot-score quality and compute cost?
For the proposed 98B scale-up, what computational memory and device count do you anticipate? Will training involve full retraining or parameter inheritance with further fine-tuning? Could you estimate the training time for the 98B version runs?
Can you provide some other evaluation metrics, such as some biophysical indicators, to prove the learning of topological structures?
As for the experiment part, can you provide your technique for split training and validation sets in detail? And could you please provide a more comprehensive experiment on the same dataset among different models?

Application of ESM3 to Knotted Protein Design with Interesting Results but Limited Methodological Novelty
Official Reviewby Reviewer Qboz30 May 2025, 17:14 (modified: 08 Jun 2025, 02:46)Program Chairs, Area Chairs, Reviewers Submitted, Reviewer Qboz, AuthorsRevisions
Review:
Summary
This paper applies the recently released ESM3 multi-modal protein language model to the problem of knotted protein design. The authors propose a continuous knot score to measure topological robustness, demonstrate guided generation of knotted proteins with a significantly improved success rate (87%), and achieve accurate classification (93%) using ESM3 embeddings. They also show that unknotted proteins can be transformed into knotted variants through iterative modifications. Overall, the study demonstrates that existing large-scale protein models can be effectively repurposed for rare topology design.

Strengths
Addresses a unique and biologically interesting problem: knotted protein generation.
Demonstrates practical use of ESM3 for structure-aware guided generation.
The proposed continuous knot score provides a more nuanced measure of topological robustness.
Achieves large improvements over previous approaches in terms of generation success rate.
The experiments are organized and results are clearly presented.
All codes will be available as promised for reproduction.
Weaknesses
The work uses the pretrained ESM3-SM model entirely off-the-shelf as the embedding for a certain protain, without any architectural or training modifications.
The only introduced model is a two-layer a fully connected neural network with two hidden layers for classification.
The guided generation procedure is built on existing mechanisms and an externally defined scoring function, limiting novelty in modeling.
There is limited comparison between the proposed method and previous methods, and no ablation experiment of your method (suggest to put in appendix). It is uncertain how much your proposed sampling method (using your proposed knot score) contributes to the success rate, or simply the ESM3 embedding is more powerful than previous diffusion model for protain generation. (Main concern). Not sure if previous diffusion models support your guided sampling, and what are their results using your sampling method.
(If the ESM3 embedding contributes mainly to the results) While the results are impressive, the paper would benefit from deeper analysis or explanation of why ESM3 embeddings enables such a large performance gain. It remains unclear whether this stems from its structural reasoning capabilities, its pretraining data distribution, or the guided sampling setup. More insights into which aspects of ESM3 embeddings are responsible for the improvement would strengthen the contribution.

Review of Structural Persistence Despite Sequence Redaction: A Biosecurity Evaluation of Protein Language Models
Official Reviewby Reviewer ehvs13 Oct 2025, 12:41Program Chairs, Area Chairs, Reviewer ehvs, Authors
Review:
summary
This paper examines whether modern protein generative models can reconstruct structural features when only partial sequence information is available.
Using protein knots as a non-functional but structurally complex proxy, the authors show that even with up to 80–85% sequence redaction, models such as ESM3-SM can still recover correct topologies.
The study introduces RedactBench, a benchmark for evaluating structural persistence under sequence masking, and argues that traditional redaction-based biosecurity measures may no longer be reliable in the era of generative AI.

Strengths
The paper addresses an important and emerging biosecurity issue: whether information redaction remains an effective safeguard in the era of generative biological models. The authors formalize this problem within a clear experimental framework.
The experimental design is elegant and ethically sound. By selecting “protein knots” as a complex but non-functional structural feature, the authors are able to examine model capability on topological complexity while avoiding any biological functionality or risk.
The proposed RedactBench provides a systematic methodology for testing structural persistence across masking ratios. It has potential long-term utility for the community in evaluating model robustness and safety.
The paper connects empirical findings to policy implications for biosecurity, offering a bridge between computational modeling and governance.
Weaknesses and Questions
The study focuses on topological persistence rather than biological function. While knots provide a safe test case, the link between topology and functional risk remains indirect. Extending the benchmark to include functional motifs would improve the practical significance.

The experiments rely solely on ESM3-SM, which may limit generality. Including results from additional architectures or models would help determine whether the observed effects depend on specific model designs or are general phenomena.

The dataset of 1,000 knotted and 4,000 unknotted proteins lacks detailed description (e.g., sequence length, homology, diversity). Analyses correlating these factors with reconstruction success would improve clarity.

The study adopts only a random masking strategy, which may overestimate model performance and fail to reflect realistic scenarios. The experiments should also include alternative masking methods such as contiguous segment masking and functional domain masking (based on algorithmic detection or prior knowledge) to better simulate practical redaction cases.

The authors should provide additional implementation details for Algorithm 1, including key parameters such as the number of trials (N) and the similarity threshold used to evaluate reconstruction success.

In Section 4.4, the authors claim “we observe non-linear degradation in reconstruction success,” but only one example is provided. It remains unclear whether this is a general trend or an isolated case. The authors should quantify this phenomenon and present aggregated results.

The paper should include a more detailed description of RedactBench

Review:
This paper provides a systematic biosecurity evaluation showing that even heavily redacted protein sequences can be reconstructed by modern protein language models (ESM3) into structurally accurate, sometimes functionally complex forms. It introduces a quantitative masking–recovery benchmark (RedactBench) to assess the limits of current redaction-based safety practices.

pros:

The study is timely and original, addressing a real policy-relevant biosecurity question that has not been quantified before.
The experimental design is rigorous, using both topological analysis and structural similarity metrics to validate findings.
The results are clear and impactful, demonstrating that partial sequence redaction may not be an effective safety measure.
cons:

The paper could explain more clearly how RedactBench would be implemented in practice for safety evaluations.
A few small style issues (e.g., inconsistent use of percentages and spaces in figures/tables).
Rating: 7: Good paper, accept
